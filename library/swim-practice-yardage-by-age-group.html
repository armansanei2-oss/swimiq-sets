How Much Yardage Should Age-Group Swimmers Do?

Guide · Arman Sanei

Why the Question Needs a Range, Not a Number

Parents want a number because it feels like proof of a good program. Coaches want a number because it's a fast answer at a pool deck meeting. But yardage by itself doesn't tell you much. A 10-year-old doing 2,500 yards of drill-heavy, low-intensity work is in a completely different place than one doing 2,500 yards of broken sprints. Use the ranges below as a starting reference, then adjust for the swimmer in front of you.

10&Under

  • Per practice: 1,500 to 2,500 yards
  • Per week: 4,500 to 10,000 yards across 3 to 4 sessions

At this age, technique and body awareness matter more than volume. Most of the practice should be stroke work, starts, turns, and games that build feel for the water. Aerobic work should be short and broken — 25s and 50s with rest — not long steady swims. A 10&Under swimmer who can hold a legal, efficient stroke for 25 yards is further ahead than one who can grind out 400s with a stroke that falls apart at the 200 mark.

11-12

  • Per practice: 2,500 to 4,000 yards
  • Per week: 10,000 to 18,000 yards across 4 to 6 sessions

This is the age where you start layering in real aerobic sets and introduce all four training zones in a light way — some aerobic base work, some threshold, occasional short VO2 max sets, and technique-focused sprint work. Sessions per week matter more than yardage per session here. A group swimming 3,000 yards four times a week is building more consistent aerobic capacity than one swimming 6,000 yards twice a week, even though the weekly total might be similar. Frequency drives adaptation at this age; single long sessions mostly drive fatigue.

13-14

  • Per practice: 3,500 to 5,500 yards
  • Per week: 18,000 to 30,000 yards across 5 to 8 sessions (including doubles for advanced swimmers)

Growth spurts and puberty change things fast. Two swimmers born four months apart can have completely different training tolerance. This is where you start seeing real separation between kids who can absorb higher volume and kids who need more recovery to hold technique. Aerobic threshold work becomes a bigger piece of the week, but sprint mechanics and race-pace work still need dedicated time — don't let volume crowd out speed.

Senior / National-Level Age Group

  • Per practice: 4,000 to 7,000 yards (short course), more for distance specialists
  • Per week: 30,000 to 50,000+ yards across 8 to 11 sessions, doubles 2 to 4 times

At this level, volume is individualized by event group. A 200 fly swimmer and an 800 free swimmer training in the same lane will still need different weekly totals and different ratios of aerobic to anaerobic work. Yardage here is a tool to hit specific zone targets — aerobic base, threshold, VO2 max, and speed — not a target in itself.

Sessions Per Week Beats Session Length

If you're deciding where to add training time, add a session before you add distance to existing sessions. Four 3,000-yard practices build more consistent aerobic adaptation than two 6,000-yard practices, and they're easier to recover from. This holds at every age group. Longer sessions past a certain point produce diminishing returns — technique degrades, stroke count climbs, and the last third of practice does less good than the first two-thirds.

Building Volume Across a Season

Don't jump a group from 8,000 to 20,000 yards a week in September. Build gradually:

  • Start the season at 70-80% of peak volume to re-establish aerobic base and technique.
  • Increase weekly volume by no more than 10% every one to two weeks.
  • Hold volume steady for a full week every third or fourth week to let the group absorb the load.
  • Taper volume down 3 to 4 weeks before championship meets while intensity goes up.

This is the same progressive-overload logic that applies to any endurance sport. Sudden jumps in volume are where overuse injuries and flat, unmotivated swimmers show up.

Warning Signs You've Gone Too Far

Watch for these regardless of what your log says the yardage should be:

  • Technique breakdown late in practice — stroke count rising, distance per stroke dropping, kids "surviving" sets instead of swimming them.
  • Flat affect — a group that's usually chatty and competitive goes quiet, slow to start sets, no push on the last rep of a descending set.
  • Nagging shoulder or knee complaints — especially shoulder impingement symptoms that show up mid-practice and don't resolve with a day off.
  • Falling times in-season without an obvious cause — sometimes this is just training effect, but paired with the signs above it usually means the training load has outrun recovery.

Any one of these is worth a conversation and maybe a lighter week. None of them show up in a yardage total, which is exactly why the number on the whiteboard is only ever a starting point.
