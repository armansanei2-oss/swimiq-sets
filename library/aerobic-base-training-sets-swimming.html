Aerobic Base Sets for Competitive Swimmers

Guide · Arman Sanei

What Base Work Actually Does

Aerobic base training builds the engine everything else runs on: mitochondrial density, capillary networks, fat utilization at moderate effort, and the ability to hold stroke technique when the legs and shoulders start to load up. It is not about swimming slow for the sake of it. It is controlled, repeatable effort that trains the aerobic system specifically, without tipping into anaerobic buildup.

Done right, base work sets up everything that follows. A swimmer who skips it and jumps straight into threshold and VO2 work in October usually stalls out by February — no foundation to absorb the harder work.

Knowing You're in the Right Zone

Heart rate is useful but not required. A simpler set of markers works for most pools:

  • Pace control: the swimmer holds within 1-2 seconds of target pace across the whole set, not just the first few reps.
  • Stroke count: distance per stroke stays within 1-2 strokes of the swimmer's baseline across the set. If stroke count climbs steadily, the effort has drifted out of aerobic range.
  • Recovery breathing: at the wall, the swimmer can answer a question in a full sentence within 15-20 seconds. If they're doubled over gasping, the set was run too hard for its purpose.
  • Heart rate: for swimmers you track this with, post-repeat HR in the 130-160 bpm range (age-dependent) is a reasonable aerobic-base target. This is a guide, not a rule — pace and stroke count matter more day to day.

If any of these markers breaks down early in a set, the interval is too tight or the pace is too fast for what you're trying to train.

Typical Set Shapes

Long descending freestyle. Repeats of 200-500m, descending in blocks of three or four. This teaches pace control under mild fatigue without forcing an anaerobic response.

Broken swims. A long swim broken into segments with short rest — for example a 600 broken every 100 at :10 rest. This lets a swimmer accumulate aerobic volume at a slightly faster pace than they could hold unbroken, without the rest being long enough to fully recover and turn it into a speed set.

Pull work with paddles or a band. Removes the kick, isolates the upper body, and adds light resistance. Good for reinforcing distance per stroke and catch mechanics at aerobic effort, especially for swimmers who tend to over-kick and mask a weak pull.

Aerobic IM. All four strokes at moderate, sustainable effort. Builds general aerobic fitness, keeps non-primary strokes from atrophying in the off-season, and exposes technical breakdowns in each stroke before they get baked in under fatigue.

Interval and Rest Guidance

Base sets should use rest short enough to keep the set continuous in feel but long enough that the swimmer isn't gasping — typically 10-20 seconds per 100 of work. If you're building a straight repeat set, aim for an interval that gives roughly :10-:15 rest at the target pace. Too much rest turns an aerobic set into a series of sprints with a nap in between.

Main set volume during base phase usually runs 2500-6000m depending on level and age group, with total practice volume built around that main set plus warm-up, drill, and cool-down work.

How Long to Stay in Base

Four to eight weeks of dedicated base work is standard heading into a season, longer for younger or less experienced swimmers who need more time to build aerobic capacity before they can handle threshold work without technique breakdown. Once swimmers can hit descending sets with stable stroke count and fast recovery, it's time to start layering in aerobic threshold and VO2 work — base doesn't disappear, but it stops being the primary focus.

Example Sets

Age group (12-14, developing aerobic base) Warm-up: 400 easy free/back Main: 8×200 free @3:00, descend 1-4 and 5-8, hold stroke count within 2 of baseline Then: 4×100 pull @1:40, focus on catch Intent: teach pace control and reinforce that faster doesn't mean shorter strokes.

High school / senior Main: 800 broken every 200 @:10 rest, then 5×400 free @5:30 descend 1-3 Intent: the broken 800 builds aerobic power at a pace slightly faster than straight swimming; the 400s train pace holding under mild fatigue.

Elite / college Main: 16×100 free @1:10, hold 1:04-1:06, then 3×1000 pull with band @13:00 each, negative split Intent: 100s at 1:10 with a 1:05 average leave roughly :05 rest — tight enough to stay aerobic-threshold, not anaerobic. Pull set builds upper-body aerobic capacity and reinforces even pacing over distance.

Aerobic IM (any level, adjust pace) Main: 8×100 IM @1:30, moderate effort, count strokes per 25 each round Intent: maintain technical fluency in all four strokes while banking aerobic volume — useful once a week even outside base phase.
