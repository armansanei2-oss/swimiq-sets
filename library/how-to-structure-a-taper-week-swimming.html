How to Structure a Taper Week for Swimmers

Guide · Arman Sanei

When Taper Starts and Why

For most competitive age-group and high school swimmers, taper starts 10 to 14 days out from the championship meet. Sprinters and swimmers with a smaller aerobic base often taper on the shorter end, closer to 7 to 10 days. Distance swimmers and those who've carried heavy volume all season may need the full two weeks to shed fatigue without losing fitness.

The purpose of taper isn't rest for its own sake. It's the planned reduction of training stress so the adaptations already earned in the previous months can show up on race day. Fitness doesn't improve much in taper. What improves is the ratio of fitness to fatigue.

The Volume Drop Curve

Volume should decrease gradually, not fall off a cliff. A common pattern:

  • 10-14 days out: 80-85% of peak weekly volume
  • 7-9 days out: 65-75%
  • 4-6 days out: 50-60%
  • 1-3 days out: 30-40%

A swimmer averaging 40,000 meters a week at peak might land around 32,000 in the first taper week, 24,000 in the second, and 14,000-16,000 in the final week before the meet. The drop should feel steady, not sudden. A swimmer who goes from 40,000 to 15,000 in one week usually feels flat and heavy, not sharp.

Keep Intensity, Cut Yardage

The biggest structural mistake in taper is cutting intensity along with volume. Do the opposite. Hold race-pace and above-race-pace work in the plan even as total yardage drops. This is what keeps the neuromuscular system sharp and the swimmer's sense of pace intact.

Example race-pace set for a 200 freestyler, done inside a taper-week aerobic day:

  • 6 x 75 @ goal 200 pace, on a send-off that gives 30-40 seconds rest
  • Example: goal 200 pace is 1:00 per 100, so 75 pace is 45 seconds; swim on 1:20

This is low volume (450 meters of work) but high specificity. It tells the swimmer and the coach exactly where their pace sits, without adding fatigue.

Maintaining Feel for the Water

Cutting volume too aggressively can strip away a swimmer's feel for the water — that sense of catch, connection, and rhythm that comes from accumulated repetition. Counter this with short, high-quality technical work every session: sculling, catch-up drill at speed, 25s with a focus on stroke count at race pace. Ten to fifteen minutes of this per practice keeps the stroke connected without adding meaningful load.

The Role of Speed, Starts, and Turns

Taper week is when race-day mechanics get rehearsed at full speed. Include:

  • Max-velocity swims: 4-6 x 15-25m all-out, full recovery (2-3 minutes), focused on stroke rate and turnover
  • Start work: 6-10 dive starts to a set distance (15-25m), timed if possible
  • Turn work: race-pace approach into the wall, explosive push-off, breakout to a fixed point

These are short, high-quality reps with generous rest. The goal is sharpness, not conditioning.

Sleep and Load Management

Taper is also about reducing total life stress, not just pool stress. Prioritize 8-9 hours of sleep, especially in the final 4-5 days. Dryland volume should drop alongside pool volume — this is not the week to add new strength work or increase intensity in the weight room. If a swimmer is in school, flag this week to teachers and parents if possible; a stressful exam schedule can undo a well-built taper.

Common Taper Mistakes

  • Cutting intensity instead of volume. Reducing yardage while also softening the hard efforts leaves swimmers under-stimulated and flat.
  • Over-resting sprinters. Sprinters lose feel for speed fast. They generally need shorter tapers and more frequent (though brief) high-velocity touches than distance swimmers.
  • Panic sets. Adding a big, unplanned main set three days out because a swimmer "looks tired" almost always backfires. Trust the plan you built weeks ago.
  • Too much rest, too soon. A sudden, large volume drop early in taper can leave swimmers feeling heavy and sluggish mid-week, right when confidence needs to be building.

A Day-by-Day Example Week (Final Week Before a Weekend Meet)

This assumes the meet starts Friday or Saturday. Adjust for your own competition schedule.

  • Sunday: Moderate aerobic swim, some tempo work, light. 3,500-4,000m.
  • Monday: Race-pace set (like the 6 x 75 above), some starts. 3,000-3,500m.
  • Tuesday: Short max-velocity work, turn work, easy aerobic swimming. 2,500-3,000m.
  • Wednesday: Lighter day. Technical focus, some drill, short pace work. 2,000-2,500m.
  • Thursday: Sharpening session — starts, a few race-pace 25s or 50s, easy warm-up and warm-down only. 1,500-2,000m.
  • Friday: Pre-meet shakeout if there's a morning session, or full rest if racing starts Friday evening.
  • Saturday/Race day: Standard competition warm-up, race.

Total weekly volume in this example lands around 13,000-15,500 meters, well below peak training weeks, with intensity and specificity held high throughout.

Taper Is Individual

This is a starting framework, not a prescription. Some swimmers taper better with more volume held longer; others need an early, steep drop. Track how each swimmer responds across seasons — energy levels, sleep quality, how their race-pace times feel — and adjust the curve for them specifically. The numbers above are a reasonable place to start building that history.
